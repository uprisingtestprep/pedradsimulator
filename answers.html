Pediatric Radiology Clinical Fundamentals

Answer Keys and Explanations for every Board-Style Question, organized by chapter. Question stems and answer choices are printed in each chapter of the book.

Chapter 1: Principles of Pediatric Imaging

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: ALARA means as low as reasonably achievable: justify the exam, optimize the protocol for the child's size, and avoid repeats and extra phases. Using an adult protocol ignores size-based adjustment. Blanket shielding can hide anatomy and create artifact, and repeating studies adds dose rather than removing it.
Question 2

Why the other choices are wrong: Ultrasound is real time, repeatable, and radiation free, which makes it first line for many pediatric questions. CT is fast but uses ionizing radiation. Chapter 2: Choosing the Right Imaging Test in Children

Question 1
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Ultrasound is the usual first test for suspected pediatric appendicitis because it avoids radiation and can be repeated. CT is a second step when ultrasound is inconclusive and risk stays high. MRI under anesthesia is not a first step, and an upper GI study evaluates malrotation rather than the appendix.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: The third step asks whether the result will alter treatment and whether a normal result is trustworthy, otherwise a more sensitive test should be chosen. Scanner availability, family familiarity, and number of images are not diagnostic reasoning and do not justify an examination.
Question 3
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Bilious vomiting in a young infant is malrotation with volvulus until proven otherwise, a minutes-to-hours problem. Discharge risks bowel loss. A renal study does not explain bilious vomiting, and neuroimaging is not the first test for this presentation.

Chapter 3: Radiation Protection, Sedation, and Contrast Safety

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Plain chest radiographs deliver far less radiation than CT. The doses are not identical, radiography is not larger than CT, and both use ionizing radiation, so each needs justification and optimization.
Question 2
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: ALARA starts with justification, then optimization of protocol and field, then dose management by avoiding repeated imaging, extra phases, and overlap. Sedation and shielding are not the framework, high settings and added phases increase dose, and cancellation of every risky study is not the principle.
Question 3
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: CTDIvol and DLP are scanner output metrics useful for protocol comparison and error detection. Effective dose is an estimate for populations, not a precise description of one child's risk. They are useful rather than meaningless, and they do not specify gonadal dose alone.

Chapter 4: Normal Growth, Variants, and Artifacts That Mimic Disease

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Growth plates are lucent cartilaginous lines in an expected location, symmetric with the opposite side. A fracture would cross the plate, alter alignment, or come with soft tissue swelling. A skin fold would not follow the physis, and a bone cyst would be a focal lesion rather than a symmetric line.
Question 2
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: The thymus is large and lobulated in infants and shrinks with age, and the sail sign is a normal appearance. Airway compression, calcification, or a clinically unwell child would suggest a true mass. A pneumothorax shows a visceral pleural line with absent lung markings, and teratoma typically contains calcification or fat.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Gas and fluid change with feeding and crying, and true obstruction shows dilated loops proximal to a transition with a supporting clinical course. Surgery and CT without clinical support are unjustified. Pneumatosis is intramural gas in a sick infant and not a general consequence of crying.

Chapter 5: Normal Neonatal and Infant Neuroimaging

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: The anterior fontanelle, and sometimes the mastoid region, gives access for ultrasound, so it can be done at the bedside and repeated safely. A fully ossified skull blocks sound, the orbit is not the standard window, and infants can move so cooperation still matters.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: The germinal matrix is a fragile low-echogenicity region beside the ventricles in premature infants. The choroid plexus is echogenic and can be mistaken for hemorrhage, the cavum is a midline fluid space, and the cisterna magna is a posterior fossa cistern.
Question 3
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Diffusion-weighted imaging identifies acute injury, susceptibility sequences detect blood, and vascular sequences show thrombosis or stroke when suspected. T2 alone is insufficient, spectroscopy supplements rather than replaces anatomy, and not all neonatal injury is vascular.

Chapter 6: Congenital Brain and Spinal Malformations

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: A myelomeningocele is an open neural tube defect with exposed neural tissue, and it is commonly associated with Chiari II malformation. A meningocele contains meninges and fluid without the cord, a lipomyelomeningocele contains fat that tethers the cord, and neither is linked to callosal agenesis or lissencephaly.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Chiari II shows descent of tonsils and vermis, a low elongated fourth ventricle, and a beak-like tectum, usually with myelomeningocele. The posterior fossa cyst with elevated tentorium describes the Dandy-Walker continuum, parallel ventricles describe callosal agenesis, and the gray-matter-lined cleft is schizencephaly.
Question 3
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Absence of the callosal bridge leaves parallel separated lateral ventricles and radially oriented gyri. A single ventricle with fused deep structures is holoprosencephaly, a thick smooth cortex is lissencephaly, and a gray-matter-lined cleft is schizencephaly.

Chapter 7: Neonatal Hypoxic-Ischemic Injury and Intracranial Hemorrhage

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Partial, prolonged insults in term infants injure the watershed zones between arterial territories. Selective basal ganglia and thalamic injury is typical of a sudden severe insult instead. Germinal matrix hemorrhage is a preterm lesion, and cerebellar hemorrhage is a separate complication of prematurity.
Question 2
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: The germinal matrix lies beside the lateral ventricles and bleeds first in premature infants, giving an echogenic focus at the caudothalamic groove. Leukomalacia involves white matter away from the groove and evolves to cysts later. A choroid plexus cyst lies within the ventricle, and cerebellar hemorrhage is in the posterior fossa.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Diffusion restriction reflects acute cytotoxic swelling and appears early. Susceptibility imaging detects blood, which is not the early marker of swelling. High T2 signal in the deep nuclei appears later, and leptomeningeal enhancement points to meningitis, not hypoxic-ischemic injury.

Chapter 8: Pediatric Central Nervous System Infection and Inflammation

Question 1
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Temporal and inferior frontal swelling with early diffusion restriction is the classic herpes pattern, and treatment should not wait for a perfect image. ADEM produces multifocal lesions after a viral illness. An abscess has a ring of enhancement, and congenital cytomegalovirus is a perinatal disease with periventricular calcification.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Pus restricts diffusion and the inflamed membranes enhance, so this is empyema. A hygroma follows CSF without restriction or enhancement. A chronic hematoma shows blood products without marginal enhancement, and ventriculitis lies in the ventricles, not the subdural space.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Debris, septations, and fluid levels in the ventricles of a child with a drain or shunt indicate ventriculitis. Meningitis may be subtle and does not give intraventricular debris. Venous thrombosis and ADEM have different imaging patterns and no link to hardware.

Chapter 9: Pediatric Brain and Spinal Cord Tumors

Question 1
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: A vermian mass filling the fourth ventricle with restricted diffusion is medulloblastoma, and CSF spread makes spinal staging necessary. Pilocytic astrocytoma is cystic with a mural nodule. Ependymoma arises from the floor and squeezes through the ventricular openings, and diffuse midline glioma expands the brainstem diffusely.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: A cystic cerebellar mass with an enhancing mural nodule is the classic pilocytic astrocytoma pattern. Medulloblastoma is usually a solid vermian mass with restricted diffusion. Craniopharyngioma and germ cell tumors are midline sellar, suprasellar, or pineal lesions.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Location and age narrow the differential before contrast or surgery. Molecular subtype cannot be assigned from conventional MRI. Enhancement, calcification, and cysts are part of the later description of the mass.

Chapter 10: Pediatric Head, Neck, Airway, and Orbital Imaging

Question 1
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: Stridor, drooling, and a muffled voice suggest obstruction, so the airway comes first and the child should not be laid flat or sent for a long study. Immediate CT, supine radiography, or sedated MRI each delay or endanger an unstable airway.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: A collection between the pharynx and the prevertebral fascia is a retropharyngeal abscess. Peritonsillar abscess is in the tonsillar region, Ludwig angina involves the submandibular and sublingual spaces, and lymphadenitis is a node, not a space collection.
Question 3
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: Many aspirated objects are not radiopaque, and unilateral air trapping or mediastinal shift on inspiratory and expiratory views should prompt urgent airway assessment. A normal film does not exclude a foreign body, waiting delays treatment, and ultrasound does not image the airway.

Chapter 11: Normal Pediatric Chest and Congenital Lung Disease

Question 1
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: Bowel gas in the chest, a scaphoid abdomen, and mediastinal shift indicate diaphragmatic hernia, more often on the left. Airway malformation, sequestration, and lobar overinflation are lung lesions, not herniation of abdominal contents.
Question 2
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: The anomalous systemic artery is the key feature of sequestration, and recurrent infection in one area should prompt a search for it. An airway malformation lacks that arterial supply, a pulmonary AVM connects pulmonary artery to vein, and scimitar syndrome involves venous drainage.
Question 3
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Plain films may suggest a ring, but CT angiography or MRI is usually needed to define it. Repeating radiographs adds nothing, and neck ultrasound cannot show the intrathoracic ring.

Chapter 12: Neonatal Chest Disease and Respiratory Emergencies

Question 1
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: Low volumes, granular opacity, and air bronchograms in a premature infant indicate surfactant deficiency. Transient tachypnea shows prominent vessels and fissural fluid with mild hyperinflation, meconium aspiration is patchy with air trapping, and pulmonary hemorrhage is nonspecific opacity with bloody secretions.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Prominent vessels, fissural fluid, and quick improvement fit retained fetal lung fluid, and the diagnosis rests on the clinical course. RDS occurs in premature infants with low volumes, meconium aspiration shows air trapping, and pneumonia is focal or patchy and often does not clear quickly.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Sudden worsening in RDS should prompt a search for air leak, interstitial emphysema, infection, or tube malposition. Gradual clearing is expected after surfactant, transient tachypnea does not follow RDS, and the thymus does not cause sudden deterioration.

Chapter 13: Pediatric Pneumonia, Tuberculosis, and Diffuse Lung Disease

Question 1
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: A clear effusion without septations beside consolidated lung is a simple parapneumonic effusion, which often clears with antibiotics. Empyema would show septations or loculation and often needs drainage. Necrotizing pneumonia shows multiple small cavities in the lung, and a lung abscess is a thick-walled cavity with an air-fluid level, neither of which is described.
Question 2
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: Ultrasound separates fluid from consolidated lung and shows septations without radiation. Contrast CT is reserved for complex cases, suspected necrosis, or procedure planning rather than routine use. A decubitus film shows free layering fluid but cannot reliably show septations, and MRI is not a standard first test for pleural disease.
Question 3
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Empyema is infected pleural fluid that can loculate and may need drainage. A pneumatocele is a thin-walled air collection, a lung abscess is a thick-walled pus-filled cavity within the lung, and necrotizing pneumonia is lung tissue death with multiple small cavities. All three are parenchymal rather than pleural.

Chapter 14: Congenital Heart Disease and Pediatric Cardiovascular Imaging

Question 1
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Transposition connects the aorta to the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery to the left ventricle, so survival depends on mixing, and the mediastinum appears narrow. Tetralogy gives a boot-shaped heart with reduced vascularity. Obstructed total anomalous venous return mimics lung disease with edema, and coarctation causes arm hypertension and weak femoral pulses.
Question 2
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: Tetralogy of Fallot combines a ventricular septal defect, pulmonary outflow obstruction, an overriding aorta, and right ventricular hypertrophy, and shows a boot-shaped heart with decreased vascularity. A large ventricular septal defect and a patent ductus cause increased vascularity from left-to-right flow. An atrial septal defect is also a left-to-right shunt and is often silent in childhood.
Question 3
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: The segmental method follows veins, atria, ventricles, great arteries, and their connections, then assesses pulmonary and systemic flow and mixing, so the report matches the bedside physiology. The radiograph cannot measure pressures or replace echocardiography. Heart size alone cannot classify lesions, and rib notching relates to coarctation rather than identifying an operation.

Chapter 15: Pediatric Mediastinal Masses and Chest Trauma

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Lymphoma is a common anterior or middle mediastinal mass in older children and can compress the trachea and superior vena cava. Neuroblastoma is posterior and affects younger children, and a bronchogenic cyst is a middle mediastinal fluid-filled lesion. A large normal thymus explains a smooth anterior opacity in an infant, not a symptomatic adolescent.
Question 2
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: The first concern is airway or venous compromise, because a child can look well while lying flat yet be at risk of collapse. Calcification may narrow the differential but does not replace pathology. Position behind the heart and immunization history do not determine immediate safety.
Question 3
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Neuroblastoma is a posterior mediastinal tumor of young children that may calcify and extend toward the spine. Teratoma and thymic lesions are anterior, and lymphoma is anterior or middle. The report should describe spinal canal extension, airway effect, and vessel relationships.

Chapter 16: Neonatal Gastrointestinal Obstruction and Abdominal Emergencies

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Bilious vomiting in a newborn is a surgical emergency until malrotation with volvulus is excluded, and a normal radiograph does not rule it out. Duodenal atresia typically shows a double bubble, meconium ileus shows distal obstruction related to cystic fibrosis, and Hirschsprung disease causes distal functional obstruction.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Duodenal atresia produces gas in the stomach and proximal duodenum with little distal gas, the double bubble sign. A coiled tube in an upper pouch suggests esophageal atresia, pneumatosis and portal gas suggest necrotizing enterocolitis, and meconium pellets point to distal obstruction.
Question 3
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: Pneumatosis intestinalis, portal venous gas, and bloody stools in a premature infant indicate necrotizing enterocolitis. Malrotation presents with bilious vomiting, meconium plug syndrome causes distal obstruction, and esophageal atresia presents with feeding difficulty and a coiled tube.

Chapter 17: Acute Abdominal Pain in Older Children

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: A target-like mass with bowel within bowel is intussusception, and many cases can be reduced by air or contrast enema. Appendicitis shows a noncompressible appendix, ovarian torsion shows an enlarged ovary, and inflammatory bowel disease shows wall thickening and fat inflammation.
Question 2
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Ultrasound is often the first test because it avoids radiation. CT is used when ultrasound is inconclusive and risk stays high, and MRI is an option in selected centers. A plain radiograph cannot reliably show the appendix.
Question 3
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Perforation, peritonitis, or an unstable child make enema reduction unsafe, so these are checked first. Appendix size is irrelevant, a lead point such as a lymph node does not prevent reduction, and the classic triad or currant jelly stool is not always present.

Chapter 18: Hepatobiliary, Splenic, and Pancreatic Disease

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: A small gallbladder with a triangular cord sign and pale stools raises concern for biliary atresia, where early surgery improves outcomes. A choledochal cyst shows duct dilation, physiologic jaundice occurs in the first days of life, and a hemangioma is a vascular lesion.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: A normal-sized gallbladder does not exclude biliary atresia, and persistent jaundice needs a timely pathway including a hepatobiliary scan. The gallbladder is not always absent, and normal size does not prove bile drainage.
Question 3
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: A hepatobiliary scan shows whether bile reaches the bowel. A radiograph does not show bile, a contrast enema evaluates the colon, and a chest radiograph cannot assess bile flow.

Chapter 19: Congenital Anomalies of the Kidney and Urinary Tract

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Posterior urethral valves obstruct the outlet in boys and produce bilateral hydronephrosis with a thick-walled bladder. Bilateral reflux alone does not thicken the bladder wall. Pelviureteric junction obstruction leaves the bladder normal. Prune belly syndrome has a typical abdominal wall deficiency and is far less common.
Question 2
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Hydronephrosis only describes dilation and may be transient or physiologic. Obstruction must be established by context and follow-up. Cortical thinning is a separate finding. Backward flow is reflux, a different process.
Question 3
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Ultrasound is the radiation-free first test, and waiting a few days avoids false reassurance from low urine output. Routine cystography in all infants is not required. A scan on day one is unreliable because of immature renal function. Computed tomography exposes the infant to radiation without need.

Chapter 20: Urinary Tract Infection, Reflux, and Obstructive Uropathy

Question 1
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: Ultrasound is radiation-free and finds obstruction, anomalies, and larger complications. Routine voiding cystography after a first simple infection is not recommended. A DMSA scan during acute illness cannot separate temporary change from scar. Computed tomography adds radiation and is reserved for complicated cases.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Failure to improve suggests focal bacterial nephritis or abscess, which contrast-enhanced imaging can show and define. Waiting without imaging risks missing an abscess. Reflux grading does not explain lack of response. MAG3 renography measures drainage, not an infected focus.
Question 3
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: DMSA binds functioning proximal tubular cells, and a persistent defect months later indicates scar. Early DMSA cannot tell reversible change from scar. MAG3 evaluates drainage. A voiding study shows reflux, not scar.

Chapter 21: Renal, Adrenal, and Pelvic Tumors in Children

Question 1
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: A well-demarcated intrarenal mass in a healthy toddler with a claw of normal kidney is typical of Wilms tumor. Neuroblastoma arises outside the kidney and encases vessels. Mesoblastic nephroma is a neonatal tumor. Renal cell carcinoma is uncommon at this age.
Question 2
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Calcification and vessel encasement from an extrarenal mass point to neuroblastoma. Wilms tumor displaces vessels. Adrenal hemorrhage involutes over weeks and does not encase vessels. Adrenocortical carcinoma usually shows hormonal effects.
Question 3
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: Wilms tumor is intrarenal with a claw of renal tissue and vessel displacement; neuroblastoma is extrarenal, calcifies, and encases. The reversed statement is wrong. Age ranges given are incorrect. Wilms tumor is usually unilateral.

Chapter 22: Pediatric Pelvic and Gynecologic Imaging

Question 1
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: An enlarged ovary with peripheral follicles and acute pain indicates torsion; preserved Doppler flow does not exclude it. A ruptured cyst spares the ovary's flow. Appendicitis is a separate bowel finding. Pelvic inflammatory disease occurs with fever and sexual activity.
Question 2
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Partial or intermittent torsion and dual blood supply can preserve flow, so clinical findings and enlarged size still count. The statements about small ovaries, masses only, and simultaneous flow loss are not accurate.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Before puberty the uterus is tubular with a cervix equal to or larger than the fundus, and ovaries are small. The pear shape and thick stripe appear at puberty. Both organs can usually be seen.

Chapter 23: Normal Skeletal Development and Pediatric Fracture Patterns

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: A subtle spiral tibial shaft fracture in a toddler is the toddler fracture. Tibial plateau fractures need high energy. Osgood-Schlatter is an overuse problem in adolescents. A Salter-Harris injury involves the growth plate and has a different pattern.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Type IV crosses the metaphysis, physis, and epiphysis, so it enters the joint and may arrest growth. Type I is physeal only. Type II exits through the metaphysis. Type V is a crush injury of the physis.
Question 3
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: Type II is the most common and has a metaphyseal corner fragment. Type I is physeal only, type III involves the physis and epiphysis, and type IV involves all three layers; each is less frequent.

Chapter 24: Skeletal Dysplasias, Metabolic Bone Disease, and Marrow Disorders

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Widened, cupped, frayed metaphyses and widened physes are the signs of rickets. Scurvy produces dense lines and a ring epiphysis. Achondroplasia is a dysplasia with a different pattern. Osteogenesis imperfecta shows fragile osteopenic bones and fractures.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Rickets appears first where growth is fastest, at the wrists and knees. The skull, small tubular bones, and axial skeleton are not the early sites.
Question 3
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: Failure of the interpediculate distance to widen caudally with rhizomelic shortening is achondroplasia. Morquio and spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia have short trunks. Thanatophoric dysplasia is lethal with a small thorax.

Chapter 25: Hip, Limb, and Growth-Plate Disorders

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Ultrasound shows the unossified cartilaginous head, its coverage by the socket, and stability with stress, so it is the first test. A radiograph shows little because the head is not ossified. MRI needs sedation and is not a screening tool, and CT adds radiation without showing cartilage well.
Question 2
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Perthes disease is avascular necrosis of the femoral epiphysis, and early radiographs may be normal while MRI shows marrow change. Slipped capital femoral epiphysis is a disease of older, often obese adolescents. Dysplasia is a developmental problem seen in infants, and septic arthritis presents with fever and inability to bear weight.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Slipped capital femoral epiphysis can present as referred knee pain, so a normal knee film calls for hip imaging, including a lateral view. Knee MRI would look at the wrong joint. Waiting two weeks delays diagnosis of a slip that can become unstable, and knee ultrasound again targets the wrong joint.

Chapter 26: Bone and Joint Infection, Inflammation, and Chronic Disease

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Radiographs often lag by weeks, and MRI detects early marrow edema, abscess, and joint involvement. Normal films do not exclude infection. Ultrasound shows fluid beside bone but not marrow, and periosteal reaction appears later rather than being a prerequisite.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: A sequestrum is dead bone surrounded by inflammation, and a sinus tract may drain to the skin. An involucrum is the reactive new bone shell rather than dead bone. A physeal bridge follows plate injury, and the fallen fragment sign belongs to a fractured unicameral bone cyst.
Question 3
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: Fever, a painful flexed hip, and refusal to walk suggest septic arthritis, where delay damages cartilage and growth plate. Transient synovitis is a diagnosis of exclusion. Juvenile arthritis is chronic, and Perthes disease is not an acute febrile illness.

Chapter 27: Pediatric Bone and Soft Tissue Tumors

Question 1
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Aggressive bone formation, destruction, and periosteal reaction in the metaphysis of an adolescent indicate osteosarcoma. A nonossifying fibroma is benign with a sclerotic rim. A unicameral cyst is fluid-filled, and an osteochondroma is a bony projection with a cartilage cap.
Question 2
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Ewing sarcoma favors the diaphysis, can have a large soft-tissue mass, and mimics infection, so biopsy and staging are needed. Osteosarcoma is typically metaphyseal in adolescents. Nonossifying fibroma is benign, and Langerhans disease often needs biopsy, so it is not always self-limited.
Question 3
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: A well-defined metaphyseal lucency with a sclerotic rim and no aggressive features is a nonossifying fibroma. The other options are malignant or infectious and would show a wide zone of transition, cortical destruction, or periosteal reaction.

Chapter 29: Whole-Body Pediatric Trauma and Emergency Radiology

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Bedside ultrasound (FAST) detects free fluid quickly without delaying resuscitation. CT requires a stable child and transport, and is not the first test in an unstable patient. Radiographs are insensitive for free fluid, and MRI is far too slow and sedation-dependent for this setting.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Contrast-enhanced CT shows lacerations, hematoma, active extravasation, and pseudoaneurysm, which guide grading and management. Question 3
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: A dedicated skeletal survey with separate coned views is the standard for suspected abuse in young children, and a follow-up survey can reveal fractures initially occult. A babygram is not diagnostic quality. Whole-body CT delivers excess dose and misses classic metaphyseal lesions, and scintigraphy alone is not the standard first test.

Chapter 30: Staging, Surveillance, and Complications of Childhood Cancer

Question 1
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Neuroblastoma commonly calcifies, encases vessels, crosses the midline, and elevates catecholamine metabolites. Wilms tumor displaces vessels and rarely calcifies. Hepatoblastoma is liver-based with raised alpha-fetoprotein, and rhabdomyosarcoma does not produce catecholamines.
Question 2
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: MIBG scans show soft-tissue and bone disease in MIBG-avid neuroblastoma, and CT or MRI define the primary and its vascular relationships. Gallium and a skeletal survey are outdated. Chest radiograph misses most disease, and PET without anatomic correlation is inadequate.
Question 3
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: The claw sign shows the mass arises from the kidney, which supports Wilms tumor, and staging must assess venous extension and the other kidney. Neuroblastoma arises outside the kidney. Cystic nephroma is a different cystic lesion, and tumor thrombus is not simple bland thrombosis.
